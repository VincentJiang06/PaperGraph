# Position-space

## P1: Demographic / Malthusian factor-price
holder: M.M. Postan (and the neo-Malthusian E. Le Roy Ladurie)
claim: The post-plague population collapse alone drove serfdom's end, because a halved labour supply mechanically raised real wages, depressed grain prices and land values, and forced lords to commute servile dues and bid for scarce free labour.
rests_on: English manorial real-wage and rent series showing wages rising and grain prices/land values falling after 1348–1375, read as a secular Malthusian population–resource cycle.

## P2: Class-power / balance of class forces (political Marxist)
holder: Robert Brenner
claim: Because the same secular demographic trend produced opposite outcomes — dissolution in England, seigneurial reaction in France's persistence and East-Elbian re-enserfment — the determining variable was the prior balance of peasant communal organisation against lord-and-state coercive power, not demography.
rests_on: The comparative "controlled experiment" of divergent institutional outcomes (England vs France vs East-Elbe) under a shared demographic shock, plus evidence of peasant solidarity and seigneurial-state strength.

## P3: Smithian / commercialization
holder: Henri Pirenne and the trade-and-towns / market-growth tradition
claim: The growth of towns, long-distance trade and a monetised economy dissolved serfdom by making money rents more attractive than labour services and by offering peasants an urban exit ("town air makes free").
rests_on: Correlation of serfdom's decline with commercial and urban density in the West, the spread of money-rent commutation, and the contrasting weakness of towns in the East.

## P4: Neoclassical property-rights / institutional
holder: Douglass North and Robert Thomas
claim: Serfdom was an efficient labour-rent contract for its factor endowment, and it unravelled in the West once the post-plague rise in the land-to-labour ratio changed relative factor prices and made wage/money-rent contracts the lower-cost arrangement.
rests_on: A relative-factor-price model in which institutions track efficiency, mediated by transaction costs and the state as contract enforcer; the plague as the exogenous shock to the land:labour ratio.

## P5: Crisis of feudalism / rate of feudal levy
holder: Guy Bois (Crise du féodalisme)
claim: The dissolution reflects an endogenous crisis of the feudal mode of production — a secular fall in the rate of seigneurial levy squeezing lords — of which the demographic downturn is a symptom, not the autonomous cause.
rests_on: Eastern-Normandy manorial evidence of a long-run decline in the per-unit rate of feudal extraction and the erosion of fixed money rents, framing demography as manifestation of a structural contradiction.

## P6: East-Elbian "second serfdom" via export trade + weak towns + strong nobility
holder: Marian Małowist, Witold Kula, Jerome Blum (the East-European economic-history camp)
claim: East-Elbian re-enserfment was produced by the sixteenth-century Baltic grain-export boom, which made demesne (Gutsherrschaft) production for Western markets profitable while feeble towns and a state-capturing Junker nobility left peasants no shelter.
rests_on: The Danzig/Gdańsk grain-export trade to the West, demesne expansion and labour-service intensification, thin urbanisation and a burgher class too weak to absorb fugitives, and noble domination of the diets.

## P7: Institutional integration / the state
holder: S.R. Epstein
claim: Coercive labour institutions survived or fell according to jurisdictional fragmentation and market integration under state formation — not demography or an autonomous class balance — so freedom expanded where transaction costs and coordination failures were reduced.
rests_on: Comparative European evidence that legal-jurisdictional integration and better-integrated markets, driven by state consolidation, correlate with the erosion of servile constraints independently of population trend.

## STRONGEST-OBJECTION
claim: The thesis's linchpin — an "identical demographic shock" yielding opposite outcomes — is false, because the land-abundant, thinly-settled East never shared the West's factor experience and its serfdom hardened a century-plus later in response to the sixteenth-century Western grain boom, so different factor endowments plus market access explain both outcomes and make "class power" an endogenous by-product rather than the prior autonomous cause.
rests_on: For the thesis to fail it must be true that East and West faced materially different demographic/factor conditions (not one identical shock), that the second serfdom's timing tracks later commercial-demographic demand, and that the pre-existing "balance of class power" was itself shaped by those economic forces rather than exogenous to them.
