# The Black Death and the Divergent Fate of Serfdom: Demography Through the Balance of Class Power

thesis: In Western Europe the dissolution of serfdom after the Black Death (c. 1348–1500) was
driven primarily by the demographic collapse that raised the scarcity and bargaining power of
peasant labour; but because the identical demographic shock entrenched a second serfdom in
Eastern Europe, the divergent outcomes show demography operated only through pre-existing
balances of class and political power, not as an autonomous cause.
field-weight: humanities

## The puzzle

The Black Death killed on the order of a third to a half of the population wherever it struck
[b1], and by the crudest economic logic that shock should have done one thing everywhere:
made labour scarce, made land abundant, and shifted the terms of the lord–peasant relation in
the peasant's favour. In the West it broadly did. In the East, over the following century and
a half, the very opposite happened — labour services intensified and a "second serfdom" was
fastened onto the peasantry. Any adequate account must explain both outcomes from one cause,
and it is that requirement that decides among the competing schools.

## Demography as mechanism

The demographic reading is the natural starting point and, for the West, a correct one at the
level of mechanism [P1]. That labour had become scarce and dear is not an inference but a fact
recorded in law: the English Statute of Labourers of 1351 tried to force men to work at
pre-plague wage levels and forbade masters to offer more [b2], which is precisely the reflex
of lords confronting a collapsed labour supply. Where the Postan tradition overreaches is in
treating this factor-price movement as a self-sufficient cause; the same movement, as we will
see, coincided with the opposite institutional result in the East, so the mechanism cannot be
the whole explanation.

## The class-power turn

This is the decisive move, and it is Brenner's [P2]. If one secular demographic trend yields
serfdom's dissolution in England and its intensification east of the Elbe, then demography
cannot be the variable that selects the outcome; the selecting variable must be the prior
balance between peasant communal organisation and seigneurial-plus-state coercive power. The
1381 rising in England [b3] — with the abolition of serfdom among its explicit demands — is a
sign of exactly the peasant capacity for collective resistance that, in the West, made lordly
reimposition of servile dues too costly to sustain. The thesis defended here is this
Brenner-inflected synthesis: demography supplies the pressure, the balance of class power
supplies the outcome.

## Commercialization and its limit

The Smithian account attributes serfdom's decline to towns, trade, and money rents, and to the
urban exit that made "town air free" [P3]. For the West this is a real and complementary force.
But commercialization cannot be an autonomous cause of freedom, because in the East the growth
of a market — the sixteenth-century Baltic grain trade — worked in the opposite direction,
giving lords the incentive to bind labour more tightly to demesne production. Markets liberate
or enserf depending on who holds the whip hand, which is the thesis's point restated.

## Factor prices without the invisible hand

The North–Thomas property-rights model formalises the demographic mechanism: a higher
land-to-labour ratio changes relative factor prices and should make wage and money-rent
contracts the efficient, lower-cost arrangement [P4]. The model's elegance is also its
weakness here — applied to the East, the same rise in the land-to-labour ratio predicts the
same move toward free contracts, and the prediction fails. Institutions did not simply track
efficiency; they tracked power, which is why identical factor-price logic yields opposite
institutions on the two sides of the Elbe.

## The endogenous-crisis reading

Bois locates the cause deeper, in a secular fall in the rate of feudal levy that squeezed
lords independently of, and prior to, the demographic downturn [P5]. This is not a rival to the
thesis so much as a specification of the pressure on the seigneurial side: it explains why
lords were vulnerable, while leaving the demographic shock as the trigger and the balance of
class forces as what converts lordly weakness into peasant freedom rather than into a
redoubled squeeze.

## The Eastern case as confirmation

The East-European scholarship is where the thesis is most directly evidenced [P6]. Małowist,
Kula, and Blum explain the second serfdom by the conjunction of an export market, feeble towns
that could not absorb fugitives, and a diet-dominating nobility — that is, by a configuration of
class and political power, not by demography, which the East shared with the West. That the same
plague-era demographic world produced Gutsherrschaft here and enfranchisement there is the
central fact the thesis is built to explain.

## The strongest institutional rival

Epstein presents the sharpest alternative: that servile institutions fell where jurisdictional
fragmentation gave way to market integration under state formation, so freedom expanded with
lower transaction costs rather than with any autonomous class balance [P7]. This is a genuine
challenge, because it threatens to dissolve "class power" into state capacity. The reply is that
state and class are not separable in this period: whose transaction costs the state lowered, and
whose coercion it enforced, was itself set by the balance of social forces the thesis makes
central — the East-Elbian state served the Junkers precisely because they had captured it.

## The strongest objection, answered

The hardest objection denies the thesis's linchpin outright: there was no "identical shock,"
because the land-abundant East never shared the West's factor experience and its serfdom
hardened only a century later in response to the Western grain boom, so different endowments
plus market access — not a prior class balance — explain both outcomes [OBJ]. Two things must be
granted: the timing did differ, and factor endowments and market access were real forces. But
the objection actually concedes the thesis's core. If demography plus factor endowment plus
market access can drive serfdom up in one place and down in another, then none of those
economic variables autonomously determines the outcome; what determines whether a grain boom is
met by binding labour or by hiring it is the balance of power between lords, peasants, and the
state. The objection relocates the mechanism without displacing the conclusion: demography
mattered, but only through the political settlement it acted upon.

## Conclusion

The post-plague West did shed serfdom largely because population collapse made labour scarce and
dear; but the Eastern counter-case shows that this demographic pressure was not self-executing.
The same shock, meeting a different balance of class and state power, produced the opposite
institution. Demography loaded the gun; the balance of class power decided where it pointed.
