# Nuclear Power's Safety and Carbon Record: Ordinary Facts and Extraordinary Fears

thesis: Per unit of electricity generated, nuclear power causes far fewer deaths and far lower
lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions than fossil fuels — comparable to wind and solar — so on
realized-mortality and carbon grounds the objection to nuclear rests on catastrophic tail-risk,
waste longevity, cost, and proliferation, not on its ordinary safety or climate record.
field-weight: science

## Two questions kept apart

Public argument about nuclear power runs two questions together: how safe and clean has it
actually been, and whether we should build more of it. This paper answers only the first, and
argues that on the realized record the answer is decisively favourable — which is why the
serious case against nuclear has to be made on other grounds.

## The realized safety record

The mortality accounting is not close. Using Our World in Data's per-terawatt-hour figures,
nuclear power is associated with about 0.03 deaths per TWh [c1], while coal is associated with
about 24.62 [c2] — so that coal is on the order of 800 times deadlier than nuclear for the same
electricity [c3], once chronic air-pollution mortality and accidents across the fuel chain are
counted. Nuclear's rate also sits in the same band as the renewables usually held up as safest:
wind at about 0.035 deaths per TWh [c4] and solar at about 0.019 [c5]. This is the empirical
core of the per-TWh consensus [P1]: nuclear's death rate is two to three orders of magnitude
below fossil fuels and statistically indistinguishable from wind and solar.

## The carbon record

The climate accounting points the same way. On a lifecycle basis the IPCC's median estimate for
nuclear is about 12 gCO2eq/kWh — similar to wind and below all forms of solar [n1] — and far
beneath coal or gas. The ecomodernist camp presses this further into a counterfactual [P2]:
Kharecha and Hansen calculate that nuclear generation has already prevented on the order of 1.84
million air-pollution deaths and 64 gigatonnes of CO2 that fossil combustion would otherwise
have produced [n2]. One need not accept the full prevented-death projection to grant its
retrospective half, which follows directly from the mortality and emissions rates above.

## Where the real objections live

If the safety and carbon records are this favourable, the substantive objections must lie
elsewhere — and they do. The strongest is the tail-risk argument [P3]: that a mean of 0.03
deaths per TWh is a point estimate over a short, fortunate window, that accident severity is
heavy-tailed, and that tightly-coupled reactor systems make catastrophic failure an inherent
possibility whose latent-cancer and evacuation tolls per-kWh averages omit. This is the one
objection that engages the safety record on its own terms, and the thesis concedes its force as
a claim about the variance, not the mean. Distinct from it is the intergenerational objection
[P6]: that nuclear's signature harm is not ordinary mortality at all but millennia-long waste
with no operating deep repository, and a fuel cycle that can leak weapons-usable material. And
the economic objection is real enough that the thesis names it [P4] — even granting safety, the
Lovins-style case points to nuclear's high and rising levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) and
chronic construction overruns such as Vogtle, arguing it abates less carbon per dollar and per
year than efficiency and renewables, so the argument against building it is about cost and
speed, exactly as the thesis claims. None of these three touches the finding that nuclear's
realized deaths and emissions are low; they relocate the debate to variance, to time-horizons,
and to money.

## Is nuclear even necessary?

A further position holds that the whole question is moot because a fully renewable grid can
replace nuclear outright [P5]. Jacobson and colleagues model a 100% wind-water-solar (WWS) system
for all US energy sectors and conclude that "no natural gas, biofuels, nuclear power, or
stationary batteries are needed" [n3]. If true, nuclear's risks and costs are simply unnecessary
to bear. We treat this as an open and contested empirical question rather than a settled fact:
Clack and twenty co-authors re-evaluated exactly that model and found it "used invalid modeling
tools, contained modeling errors, and made implausible and inadequately supported assumptions"
[n4]. Whichever way that dispute resolves, it bears on whether we *need* nuclear, not on whether
nuclear's realized record is safe and low-carbon — which is the question here.

## The strongest objection, answered

The sharpest objection is that the thesis's own move is illegitimate: for nuclear specifically
the "ordinary mortality" and "catastrophic tail-risk" cannot be cleanly separated, because the
harm distribution is heavy-tailed and a deaths-per-TWh metric that counts only radiation deaths
structurally under-measures nuclear's characteristic realized harm [OBJ]. The clearest case is
Fukushima: essentially no deaths were caused by radiation, yet the protective evacuation produced
about 2,313 disaster-related deaths among evacuees [n5] — a real toll that a radiation-only count
misses entirely. Chernobyl sharpens the point, with mainstream projections of up to about 4,000
eventual radiation deaths [n6] and higher independent estimates of roughly 27,000 excess cancer
deaths [n7]. We answer this on the merits rather than by scoping it away. First, the OWID 0.03
figure already incorporates the accident tolls, and even loading the numerator with every
Fukushima evacuation death and the high-end Chernobyl estimate — tens of thousands of deaths —
spread across the tens of thousands of TWh nuclear has generated still leaves the per-TWh rate one
to two orders of magnitude below coal's. Second, much of the Fukushima evacuation mortality fell
on relocated elderly people and reflects the severity of the protective response, so it is partly
a fact about disaster policy rather than about reactor physics — a genuine harm, but one that
cuts against over-cautious evacuation as much as against nuclear. Third, the objection is
ultimately a claim about the tail and the correct denominator, which the thesis explicitly cedes
as the legitimate ground of concern. The central estimate is low; its tail is genuinely
contested; and conceding that the fight belongs at the tail is agreeing with the thesis's
division of the question, not overturning it.

## Conclusion

On the record that can actually be counted — including the accident tolls and even the evacuation
deaths — nuclear electricity is far safer and far lower-carbon than fossil fuels and comparable to
wind and solar. That conclusion does not settle whether more reactors should be built; that
depends on cost, speed, waste politics, proliferation, and whether renewables can do the job
alone, which are real and are where the debate belongs. It does mean that the case against
nuclear cannot honestly be made on its ordinary safety or climate record.

  brackets off from the safety-and-carbon question it adjudicates.
