# Position-space

## P1: Augmentation & new-work reinstatement (thesis's home camp)
holder: Autor (task framework / "new work") and Brynjolfsson (complementarity / productivity)
claim: AI automates tasks rather than whole jobs and raises the value of the human tasks that complement it while spawning entirely new work, so labor demand for reorganized jobs persists and employment restructures rather than collapses.
rests_on: historical reinstatement of new tasks after past automation (ATMs raised teller employment; most jobs today are in occupations that barely existed a century ago) and firm RCTs showing generative AI lifts productivity most for lower-skill workers (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2023).

## P2: Task-displacement / "so-so automation" & falling labor share
holder: Acemoglu & Restrepo
claim: Automation shifts the task content of production against labor and, when it is "so-so" (displacing workers without large productivity gains), it lowers the labor share, wages, and net labor demand because new-task reinstatement has been too weak since the late 1980s to offset displacement.
rests_on: the secular US labor-share decline, local-labor-market evidence that industrial robots cut employment and wages (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2020), and Acemoglu's (2024) estimate of only modest AI TFP gains, implying displacement can dominate.

## P3: Mass occupational automatability / susceptibility
holder: Frey & Osborne
claim: A large share of employment — about 47% of US jobs in Frey & Osborne (2013) — sits in highly automatable occupations, so the pace of automatability is fast and broad enough to outrun any complementary-task creation.
rests_on: occupation-level susceptibility scoring from expert ML assessments of engineering bottlenecks (perception, creativity, social intelligence).

## P4: Broad general-purpose exposure that flips incidence to high-skill work
holder: Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock / OpenAI ("GPTs are GPTs")
claim: LLMs are a general-purpose technology whose exposure is broad and — unlike prior automation — rises with wage and education (~80% of workers have ≥10% of tasks exposed, ~19% have ≥50%), so the effect lands on high-skill non-routine cognitive work, not merely "routine" tasks.
rests_on: a task-exposure rubric applied across O*NET occupations (Eloundou et al. 2023), showing exposure concentrated in higher-paid cognitive jobs.

## P5: Polarization & distributional primacy
holder: Autor & Dorn; Goos, Manning & Salomons (routine-biased technical change)
claim: Whether or not aggregate employment holds, the defining effect of automation is distributional — it hollows out middle-skill routine jobs and depresses the labor share — so framing the outcome as benign "restructuring within jobs" masks widening wage inequality.
rests_on: job-polarization and RBTC evidence across the US and Europe (Autor & Dorn 2013; Goos, Manning & Salomons 2014) alongside the falling labor share.

## P6: Too-early / diffusion-lag / macro & remote-work confounds
holder: GPT-diffusion / productivity-paradox camp (Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson; Humlum & Vestergaard)
claim: It is premature to identify any net employment effect for 2020–2025 because general-purpose technologies diffuse with long lags, early rigorous field studies find near-zero labor-market effects, and the period is heavily confounded by the pandemic, remote-work reallocation, and stimulus.
rests_on: the modern productivity-paradox / J-curve argument (Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2019) and recent null field results (Humlum & Vestergaard 2025, no significant effect of AI chatbots on earnings or hours) against a COVID-distorted labor market.

## P7: Post-work / this-time-is-different / redistribution
holder: Susskind ("A World Without Work"), Ford ("Rise of the Robots"), tech-accelerationist camp
claim: AI differs in kind from past automation because it substitutes for cognition itself and keeps improving, so human "complementary" tasks are a shrinking residual and the real issue is managing a long-run decline in labor demand through redistribution (shorter hours, UBI) rather than intra-job restructuring.
rests_on: the claim that machine capability is a moving frontier with no stable human comparative-advantage floor, plus the value judgment that policy should plan for post-scarcity.

## STRONGEST-OBJECTION
claim: The thesis's benign verdict smuggles in a stable, growing pool of human complementary tasks (supervision, orchestration, judgment), but AI is a fast-moving general technology that increasingly automates those very tasks and targets high-skill cognitive work (Eloundou), while new-task reinstatement has been weak since the late 1980s (Acemoglu–Restrepo), so if automatability outpaces genuinely new complementary demand the net effect is falling labor demand and labor share — net displacement, not restructuring.
rests_on: for the thesis to be wrong, complementary-task demand must fail to scale with automation (because those tasks are themselves automatable at the frontier or concentrate in a few workers) while automatability advances fast and broad and the historically weak reinstatement rate persists, driving the labor share and net labor demand down.
