# AI Agents and Employment, 2020–2025: Task Restructuring, Not Aggregate Collapse

thesis: In 2020–2025, AI agents built on large language models primarily automated routine
cognitive tasks while raising demand for complementary tasks of supervision, orchestration,
and judgment, so their employment effect in this window was a structural restructuring of
tasks within jobs rather than aggregate net job destruction; the net effect is conditional,
turning on the relative pace of task automatability versus growth in complementary-task demand.
field-weight: mixed

## The task-level lens

Jobs are bundles of tasks, and technology acts on tasks, not job titles. Read at the task
level, the 2020–2025 diffusion of LLM-based agents looks less like the deletion of occupations
than like a re-weighting of which tasks inside a job are done by a person and which are handed
to a tool. This paper defends the restructuring reading while treating it as a conditional
claim, not a reassurance, and it takes seriously the camps that expect a worse outcome.

## The augmentation and reinstatement case

The thesis's home camp holds that AI automates tasks while raising the value of the human
tasks that complement it and, historically, spawning entirely new work [P1]. The cleanest
2020–2025 evidence for the complementarity channel is the Brynjolfsson–Li–Raymond field
experiment on customer-support agents, where access to a generative-AI assistant raised
resolutions per hour by about 14% on average, with the gains concentrated among novice and
low-skilled workers and little effect on the most experienced [c3]. That signature — a tool
that lifts output and compresses the skill gradient rather than emptying the role — is exactly
what augmentation predicts, and it is why the within-job restructuring reading is the right
first approximation for this window.

## Displacement and the falling labor share

The strongest structural challenge is the Acemoglu–Restrepo view that automation shifts the
task content of production against labor, and that "so-so" automation — displacing workers
without large productivity gains — lowers the labor share and net labor demand when new-task
reinstatement is too weak to offset it [P2]. We concede the mechanism rather than dismiss it:
it is precisely why the thesis is stated conditionally. Restructuring is benign only if
complementary and new tasks grow at least as fast as automatable tasks disappear; if
reinstatement stays as weak as it has been since the late 1980s, the same task-level lens
predicts falling labor demand, not a soft landing.

## Occupational automatability versus task automatability

The Frey–Osborne susceptibility tradition scores whole occupations as automatable and reaches
alarming headline shares [P3]. The task-level evidence is what disciplines that estimate:
because AI acts on tasks rather than occupations, an occupation with several exposed tasks is
reorganized, not necessarily eliminated, so occupation-level susceptibility scores
systematically overstate the pace at which jobs themselves vanish. This is not a refutation of
exposure but a correction of the unit of analysis, and it is central to why 2020–2025 did not
produce the job losses occupation-level scores implied.

## Broad, high-skill exposure

The general-purpose-exposure camp complicates the comfortable word "routine": Eloundou and
colleagues find exposure is broad and rises with wage and education, with around 80% of US
workers having at least 10% of tasks exposed and roughly a fifth having at least half [P4]
[c2]. We accept this and narrow our own claim accordingly — the tasks AI reaches are not only
low-level clerical work but cognitive, well-paid work. Exposure, however, is a measure of
potential overlap, not of automation or of net job loss; broad exposure is consistent with
broad restructuring, and it raises, rather than settles, the question of whether complementary
demand keeps pace.

## Distribution, not just level

Even granting that employment levels hold, the polarization and routine-biased-technical-change
literature insists the defining effect is distributional — hollowed middle-skill work and a
depressed labor share — so that calling the outcome benign "restructuring" can mask widening
inequality [P5]. We take this as a correction to the thesis's framing, not a rebuttal of its
level claim: "no aggregate net destruction" is compatible with real distributional harm, and a
restructuring that redistributes task rents upward is still a policy problem even when the
headcount is steady.

## Too early, and confounded

Finally, the diffusion-lag camp argues it is simply premature to read any net employment effect
for 2020–2025, because general-purpose technologies diffuse with long lags and the period is
saturated by the pandemic, remote-work reallocation, and stimulus [P6]. The best current
micro-evidence supports caution: Humlum and Vestergaard, using Danish quasi-experimental
variation, estimate precise zeros for AI chatbots' effect on earnings and hours [c4]. The
aggregate series say the same thing more crudely — mean US unemployment over 2020–2025 was
about 4.8%, a figure dominated by the 2020 pandemic spike and its recovery rather than by any
AI-attributable break [c1]. Crucially, a null aggregate effect is what the restructuring thesis
predicts for an early window: tasks are being reshuffled below the resolution of the headline
numbers.

## The strongest objection, answered

The sharpest objection is that the thesis smuggles in a stable, growing pool of human
complementary tasks, when AI is a fast-moving frontier that increasingly automates supervision,
orchestration, and judgment themselves — and, per Eloundou, targets exactly the high-skill
cognitive work those tasks live in — while reinstatement has been historically weak, so if
automatability outpaces genuinely new complementary demand the net effect is displacement, not
restructuring [OBJ]. We do not claim this cannot happen; the thesis is explicitly conditional on
the relative pace of the two forces, and the objection correctly identifies the condition under
which it fails. What the 2020–2025 evidence shows is that, so far, the condition has held: the
one channel we can measure cleanly is complementarity favoring less-skilled workers [c3], the
best-identified labor-market study finds no earnings or hours effect yet [c4], and the aggregate
shows no AI break [c1] — while the genuine risk the objection names lives in the breadth and
high-skill tilt of exposure [c2], which is why the claim is a conditional structural forecast
and not a promise of a soft landing.

## Conclusion

For 2020–2025 the weight of evidence supports task restructuring within jobs over aggregate net
job destruction, but the honest form of the claim is conditional: it holds while complementary
and new-task demand keeps pace with automatable-task displacement, and the exposure data warn
that this cannot be assumed for the years after the window.

## Excluded
- [P7] out of scope: the post-work / redistribution framing turns on a long-run (>2025)
  extrapolation of automation to near-total task coverage, which this paper's 2020–2025 window
  and its stated no-long-run-extrapolation scope explicitly exclude; it is parked, not refuted.
