# Smoke paper

thesis: AI's near-term effect on employment is distributional, not a level collapse.
field-weight: mixed

## Argument

The displacement camp is right that routine-task exposure is real [P1]: measured
routine-task intensity has fallen and that pressure is genuine, but it predicts
composition change more cleanly than a net level collapse. The augmentation view [P2]
correctly notes that past general-purpose technologies raised labor demand through new
tasks, and we grant that mechanism while noting its lag. On balance the evidence favors
the distributional reading [P3]: employment levels move little while the wage share and
task mix shift, which is what the polarization record shows.

The strongest objection is that it is simply too early to measure [OBJ]: if AI's effect
has not yet entered aggregate series, any claim of "distributional" is premature. We answer
that composition shifts are visible in micro data well before they surface in aggregates,
so absence in the aggregate is expected, not disconfirming.

Our headline datum: the AI-exposure share of postings is 40% in the sample [c1].
Autor put the point plainly [c2].

## Excluded
- [P4] out of scope: this paper is about the near term; total-automation abundance is a
  long-run extrapolation we do not adjudicate here.
