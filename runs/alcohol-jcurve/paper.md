# Is There a Safe Level of Alcohol? The J-Curve as Confounding Artefact

thesis: The apparent cardiovascular benefit of moderate drinking (the "J-curve") is largely an
artefact of confounding and abstainer (sick-quitter) bias; once these are addressed with genetic
(Mendelian-randomization) and pooled individual-participant analyses, there is no cardioprotective
threshold and total harm rises roughly monotonically with intake, so from a population-health
standpoint there is no risk-free level of alcohol.
field-weight: mixed

## The finding to be explained

For decades the epidemiology of alcohol has shown a J- or U-shaped curve: light-to-moderate
drinkers appear to outlive both heavy drinkers and abstainers, especially for coronary heart
disease [P1]. The classic camp reads this as causal cardioprotection, backed by a plausible
mechanism — ethanol raises HDL and reduces platelet aggregation. The question is whether the
dip is a fact about alcohol or a fact about who ends up in the "abstainer" comparison group.

## The artefact: who is in the reference group

The thesis's central mechanism is abstainer, or sick-quitter, bias [P2]. Conventional studies
bundle into the "non-drinker" baseline a large number of former drinkers and the already-ill who
gave up alcohol precisely because they were sick; comparing moderate drinkers against that
contaminated group manufactures an apparent benefit that shrinks toward null once lifelong
abstainers are isolated. This is not a fringe worry: reclassification analyses find the
protective signal concentrated in exactly the studies that mishandle former drinkers, and the
healthier socioeconomic profile of moderate drinkers supplies further residual confounding.

## The genetic test

Because Mendelian randomization uses gene variants that are fixed at conception and unrelated to
lifestyle, it breaks the confounding and reverse-causation that plague observational designs
[P3]. Here the evidence is direct: genetic analysis shows that the apparently protective effects
of moderate intake against stroke are largely non-causal, with risk rising log-linearly and no
protective threshold [a3]. Genetically instrumented alcohol raises blood pressure and stroke
risk monotonically — the opposite of a safe dose — which is difficult to reconcile with the
causal-protection reading of the observational curve.

## From cardiovascular to total harm

Even a cardiovascular nadir would not establish a safe dose, because health is not scored on one
organ [P4]. Summed across cancers, injury, liver disease, and the rest, the global pooled
evidence finds that the level of consumption minimising total health loss is zero [a2]. The
large individual-participant pooling points the same way for mortality: the minimum all-cause
mortality risk sits around or below 100 g per week — very low — and stroke, heart failure, fatal
hypertensive disease, and aortic aneurysm all rise with intake above it [a1]. The dose that is
"safe" for the whole person, in other words, is not the moderate dose of the J-curve.

## Pattern versus dose

A different challenge holds that the mortality curve tracks how people drink, not how much —
regular wine with meals in a Mediterranean pattern versus irregular bingeing — so that dose is
the wrong axis altogether [P6]. This is a real confounder and it cuts the thesis's way as much as
against it: if beneficial "moderate drinking" is really a marker for an advantaged lifestyle
pattern, that is one more reason the dose-response benefit is not causal. What it cannot do is
rescue a protective threshold for ethanol itself, which is what a "safe level" would require.

## The residual-benefit position, and the strongest objection

The most serious remaining position concedes the bias but insists a small, real benefit survives
it at low doses, and that "no safe level" overstates a nearly flat curve while conflating
all-cause harm with cardiovascular risk [P5]. This shades into the strongest objection [OBJ]:
that for the specific endpoint of ischaemic heart disease an inverse association persists in
exactly the bias-corrected data the thesis relies on — Wood's pooled analysis found lower
myocardial-infarction risk with higher intake [a1] — and Mendelian randomization does not refute
it, since Millwood's genetic study was underpowered for myocardial infarction and returned a null
rather than a harm [a3], so coronary protection is not established to be purely artefactual. This
is the honest hard case, and three points answer it without overclaiming. First, a single-organ
inverse association cannot ground a "safe level" when the same datasets show total mortality and
multiple vascular endpoints rising with dose [a1] and total health loss minimised at zero [a2].
Second, the MI result is a null, not a demonstrated benefit; absence of genetic harm for one
endpoint is weak evidence for causal protection against it. Third, whatever small coronary signal
survives is, on these data, swamped in absolute terms by the rising stroke, cancer, and
hypertensive risks — so even granting a residual MI effect, the intake that minimises overall
harm remains at or near zero. The objection narrows the claim from "alcohol harms every endpoint"
to "alcohol has no net protective dose for total health," which is the claim the thesis actually
needs.

## Conclusion

The J-curve is best explained as confounding and abstainer bias rather than causal
cardioprotection: the genetic evidence shows no protective threshold and monotonic vascular harm,
and the pooled evidence puts the health-loss-minimising dose at or near zero. A small,
unrefuted coronary signal may remain, but it does not amount to a safe level for the whole person.
From a population-health standpoint, there is no risk-free amount of alcohol.
