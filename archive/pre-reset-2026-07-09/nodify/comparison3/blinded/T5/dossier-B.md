# Investigation: Does raising the minimum wage reduce employment? Reconciling Card-Krueger near-zero results with competitive-market disemployment, using 2015-2024 US state/city natural experiments; roles of monopsony, cost pass-through, non-wage margins; is the effect nonlinear in the size of the hike / minimum-to-median ratio? Give a calibrated judgment.

## Lines of inquiry

### L1 [orientation: neutral] — Do the best recent US natural experiments find near-zero employment effects?
  - statement: Using modern quasi-experimental designs (bunching, event studies, matched controls) on 2015-2024-era US state/city minimum-wage changes, is the employment effect on affected/low-wage workers near zero?
  - conclusion: [SUPPORTED / high] Yes at the bites the US actually implemented: the number of low-wage jobs is little changed; the median own-wage employment elasticity (OWE) is small (~-0.1 to -0.13), so workers keep the large majority of the mandated pay raise.
  - evidence:
      - Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer 2019 (NBER w25434) — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25434/w25434.pdf — "We find that the overall number of low-wage jobs remained essentially unchanged over five years following the increase."
      - Cengiz et al. 2019 — same — "the employment elasticity with respect to the minimum wage of 0.024 (s.e. 0.025) ... the 95% confidence interval rules out substantial reductions in the aggregate employment, including the baseline aggregate employment elasticity of -0.074 in Meer and West (2016)"
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (NBER w32878) — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32878/w32878.pdf — "The median OWE estimate from the analysis of 72 published studies is -0.13 ... employment reductions offset only about 13% of the potential earnings gains ... for [57 US studies] the median and mean OWE are -0.11 and -0.22"

### L1.1 [orientation: adversarial] — The Seattle counter-case: a credible NEGATIVE natural experiment
  - statement: Does at least one high-quality 2015-era natural experiment show real disemployment?
  - conclusion: [PARTIAL-SUPPORTED / medium] Yes — Seattle's $11->$13 step cut low-wage HOURS (and hiring of new entrants), the leading US negative case; but the loss is on the hours/hiring margin, is contested, and later work finds experienced incumbents roughly break even.
  - evidence:
      - Jardim et al. 2017 (NBER w23532) — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23532/w23532.pdf — "reduced hours worked in low-wage jobs by 6-7 percent, while hourly wages in such jobs increased by 3 percent. Consequently, total payroll for such jobs decreased, implying that the Ordinance lowered the amount paid to workers in low-wage jobs by an average of $74 per month per job in 2016."
      - Evans School / UW summary — https://evans.uw.edu/new-evidence-from-the-seattle-minimum-wage-study/ — "The team found evidence of a decline in the rate of hiring of low-wage workers who were not previously employed." (and for experienced workers a "net increase of around $12 per week")

### L2 [orientation: neutral] — Is monopsony/oligopsony the mechanism that reconciles near-zero with theory?
  - statement: Do imperfectly competitive (monopsonistic) labor markets explain why a binding minimum wage need not cut employment?
  - conclusion: [SUPPORTED-partial / medium-high] Strong direct evidence: the employment effect's SIGN flips with employer concentration — negative in competitive markets, ~zero-to-positive in concentrated ones. The US "near-zero average" is a mixture across market structures.
  - evidence:
      - Azar, Huet-Vaughn, Marinescu, Taska & von Wachter 2024 (ReStud; NBER w26101) — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26101/w26101.pdf — "minimum wage-induced employment changes become less negative as labor concentration increases, and are even estimated to be positive in the most highly concentrated markets. Our findings provide direct empirical evidence supporting the monopsony [model]."
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — restating Azar et al. — "in non-concentrated markets (HHI<0.25), there is a negative (but statistically insignificant) overall OWE of around -0.50. In contrast, for concentrated markets (HHI≥0.25) there is a sizable positive and statistically significant OWE of 1.8."

### L2.1 [orientation: adversarial] — Monopsony cannot be the whole story
  - statement: If pure monopsony explained everything, prices should not rise much and profits should fall — do they?
  - conclusion: [PARTIAL / medium] No single model wins: monopsony explains the flat employment but NOT the large observed price pass-through and the non-collapse of profits. The reconciliation is a blend (imperfect competition + pass-through + X-efficiency/productivity), not monopsony alone.
  - evidence:
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — "the presence of imperfect competition in the labor market offers a natural explanation for the limited employment effects but falls short in accounting for a substantial increase in output prices or a limited pass-through to firm owners in the form of reduced profits."

### L3 [orientation: neutral] — Cost pass-through to output prices as the main reconciling margin
  - statement: Do firms absorb the mandated cost mainly by raising prices (consumers pay), preserving employment?
  - conclusion: [SUPPORTED / high] Yes — pass-through to prices is typically FULL (sometimes over-shifted). Consumers, not headcount, bear most of the cost in the local-service sectors that employ most minimum-wage workers.
  - evidence:
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — "most papers find full pass-through. In other words, the unit labor cost increase is fully covered by the unit price increase; and in some cases, we see even more than full pass-through."
      - Ashenfelter & Jurajda 2021 (IZA DP 14124) — https://docs.iza.org/dp14124.pdf — a "0.2 price elasticity with respect to wage increases. Together with the 0.7 (first-stage) elasticity of wage rates with respect to minimum wages, this implies a reduced-form price pass-through of minimum-wage-induced higher costs of labor"

### L3.1 [orientation: adversarial] — Pass-through is necessary but NOT sufficient to explain zero employment
  - statement: The competitive model also predicts pass-through — and pass-through raises prices, cuts demand, and should cut employment. Why doesn't it?
  - conclusion: [KEY TENSION / medium-high] Pass-through alone predicts job loss unless output demand is nearly inelastic; the fact that employment barely moves is itself evidence AGAINST the simple competitive model and FOR low demand-elasticity + imperfect competition.
  - evidence:
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — "the competitive neoclassical model correctly emphasizes the role of price pass-through but fails to predict the lack of employment effects—except under the assumption of completely inelastic output demand."

### L4 [orientation: neutral] — Employers adjust on NON-WAGE margins (benefits, hours, hiring bar, automation)
  - statement: Do firms recoup the mandate by cutting fringe benefits, hours, raising hiring thresholds, or automating — so effective labor cost (and thus headcount effect) is muted?
  - conclusion: [SUPPORTED / medium-high] Yes, partially: measurable clawbacks on non-wage compensation (health insurance), some hours effects, and task/automation substitution for specific groups. These shrink the effective cost shock and help explain flat headcount.
  - evidence:
      - Clemens, Kahn & Meer 2018 (NBER w24635) — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24635/w24635.pdf — employer health-insurance "coverage declines offset a modest 9% of wage gains for Very Low wage occupations"
      - Lordan & Neumark 2018, Labour Economics (NBER/IZA) — https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~dneumark/Neumark%20LE.pdf — "increasing the minimum wage decreases significantly the share of automatable employment held by low-skilled workers, and increases the likelihood that low-skilled workers in automatable jobs become nonemployed or employed in worse jobs."

### L4.1 [orientation: adversarial] — The non-wage/automation margin is bounded and heterogeneous
  - statement: Are these adjustments large enough to matter, and does automation actually follow minimum-wage hikes in the biggest low-wage sector?
  - conclusion: [MIXED / medium] Bounded: benefits offset is only ~9%; in fast food (the archetypal low-wage sector) kiosk automation shows NO link to minimum-wage hikes; hours mostly don't fall conditional on employment (Seattle the exception). Automation bites narrow subgroups (older/low-skill/manufacturing), not uniformly.
  - evidence:
      - Ashenfelter & Jurajda 2021 (IZA DP 14124) — https://docs.iza.org/dp14124.pdf — "We find no association between the adoption of labor-saving touch screen ordering technology and minimum wage hikes ... not consistent with technology introduction being a channel for negative employment effects of the minimum wage."
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — on the tradable/automatable margin — "the minimum wage is likely to have a negative effect on employment in tradable sector, and manufacturing in particular ... At the same time, the effect of the minimum wage is close to zero in the non-tradable sectors (such as restaurants or retail)" [quote in Cengiz et al. 2019, w25434, echoed by Dube-Lindner]

### L5 [orientation: neutral] — Is the employment effect NONLINEAR in the size of the hike / minimum-to-median (Kaitz) ratio?
  - statement: Does disemployment appear only above some "bite" threshold (large step / high relative to local median)?
  - conclusion: [SUPPORTED direction / medium; threshold not yet cleanly located] Yes in direction: large steps (>=$2.50/hr) and high-bite settings show clearly more negative effects; but within the US range observed through ~2016 there was NO detectable heterogeneity by Kaitz bite, and threshold estimates are statistically fragile. It is a dose-response curve whose turning point is under-identified.
  - evidence:
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878), citing Clemens & Strain 2021 — "for the six states (and D.C.) raising their minimums by $2.50/hour or more, they find an OWE of -1.01, while for the states raising less the OWE estimate is 0.46" (footnote: "a very high degree of non-linearity in employment responses")
      - Cengiz et al. 2019 (w25434) — "We also find no evidence of disemployment when we consider higher levels of minimum wages." (Dube-Lindner: Cengiz "finds no heterogeneity [by Kaitz bite] for events up to 2016")

### L5.1 [orientation: adversarial] — Are the observed high-bite cases actually benign, and thresholds an artifact?
  - statement: Do even the higher-bite US cases show small effects, making the "threshold" claim fragile/false-discovery-prone?
  - conclusion: [PARTIAL / medium] Partly yes — Cengiz finds flat effects even at the higher bites in the 1979-2016 sample, and Dube-Lindner explicitly warn threshold results are fragile. The nonlinearity is theoretically compelling and shows up for very large steps, but the US has NOT cleanly observed a sustained hike far above ~55-60% of the local median, so the turning point is extrapolated.
  - evidence:
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — "further research is needed to carefully assess possible 'non-linearities' from minimum wage increases that are both large and are at high levels ... recognizing how results on thresholds may be fragile ... challenges arising from false discovery when searching for heterogeneity by thresholds."

### L6 [orientation: adversarial — RED-TEAM the leading "near-zero" hypothesis] — The preponderance of estimates is NEGATIVE
  - statement: Is the "employment effect is ~zero" reading a selective summary? What does the full body of estimates say about the SIGN?
  - conclusion: [GENUINE TENSION, then reconciled / medium] The sign is on balance negative — most estimates are negative, and more so for teens/young/less-educated and directly-affected workers. BUT this reconciles with L1 once you separate SIGN from MAGNITUDE: most estimates are negative AND small. "Slightly negative on average, small in magnitude at observed bites" satisfies both camps.
  - evidence:
      - Neumark & Shirley 2022, "Myth or Measurement" — https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~dneumark/MW%20US%20literature.05.pdf — "79.3% of the estimated employment elasticities are negative, 55.4% are negative and significant at the 10% level or better, and 47.9% are negative and significant at the 5% level or better."
      - Neumark & Shirley 2022 — same — "this evidence is stronger for teens and young adults as well as the less-educated; ... the evidence from studies of directly-affected workers points even more strongly to negative employment effects"
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — reconciling magnitude — an "OWE that is less negative than -0.4 [represents] either a 'small negative' or 'positive' effect on jobs ... 51 (or around 71%) of the published studies have positive or small negative estimates"

### L7 [orientation: neutral — converge] — Calibrated national dose-response & who bears the loss
  - statement: For a LARGE, high-bite national hike, what is the calibrated point estimate and range, and which workers are most exposed?
  - conclusion: [CALIBRATED / medium-high] A near-doubling federal hike ($7.25->$15) yields a NEGATIVE but modest central estimate with a confidence range straddling zero — exactly the Card-Krueger/competitive tension, now dose-dependent. Losses concentrate on teens, less-educated, tradable-manufacturing, and older low-skill/automatable workers.
  - evidence:
      - CBO 2019 (via verbatim restatement, cbo.gov PDF 403s to fetch tools) — https://kinder.rice.edu/urbanedge/15-minimum-wage-could-lift-13-million-out-poverty-and-potentially-cost-13-million-jobs — "The 1.3 million Americans that could lose their jobs ... is a median estimate ... The CBO economists wrote that resulting job losses could range between 'about zero and 3.7 million.'" and "would increase the pay of at least 17 million people."
      - Dube & Lindner 2024 (w32878) — heterogeneity — "the 15 teen studies had a median OWE of -0.17 and a mean of -0.25"

## Root conclusion
[Calibrated: "Little to modest at the bites the US actually tried; genuinely negative and larger at high bites — the honest answer is a dose-response curve, not yes/no." / MEDIUM-HIGH confidence]

Within the range of minimum-wage increases the US actually implemented at the state/city level in ~2015-2024 (bites up to roughly 55-60% of the LOCAL median wage), raising the minimum wage reduced employment little: the sign is, on balance, slightly negative, but the magnitude is small — a median own-wage employment elasticity around -0.1 to -0.13, meaning affected workers retain roughly 85-90% of the mandated earnings gain. The apparent Card-Krueger vs. competitive-model contradiction largely DISSOLVES on three moves: (1) SIGN ≠ MAGNITUDE — most estimates are negative (Neumark-Shirley: 79% negative) AND small (Dube-Lindner: 71% "small negative or positive"), so both literatures are describing the same modestly-negative, small-magnitude reality; (2) labor markets are imperfectly competitive — employer concentration mediates the effect, flipping it from negative in competitive markets to zero/positive in concentrated ones (Azar et al.), so the textbook competitive prediction of large losses is simply the wrong model at moderate bites; (3) firms absorb the shock through channels OTHER than headcount — near-full price pass-through to consumers (Dube-Lindner; Ashenfelter-Jurajda), partial clawback of non-wage compensation (health insurance offsets ~9% of gains, Clemens-Kahn-Meer), some hours/hiring adjustment (Seattle), productivity/X-efficiency gains, and task substitution for narrow vulnerable groups (Lordan-Neumark) — while automation in the largest low-wage sector (fast food) shows no link to hikes (Ashenfelter-Jurajda). Crucially, the effect IS nonlinear in the bite: very large steps (>=$2.50/hr) and high-relative-to-median settings show clearly negative effects (Clemens-Strain OWE -1.01; Seattle hours loss; CBO's negative tilt for a national near-doubling), whereas moderate hikes do not. So: does raising the minimum wage reduce employment? At moderate bites — barely, if at all (near-zero to small negative). At high bites / large steps / relative to a low local median — yes, meaningfully, and the losses concentrate on teens, the less-educated, tradable-manufacturing, and older low-skill/automatable workers. No monopsony-only or competitive-only story fits; the data demand a blended, dose-dependent model.

## Open gaps
  - No clean US natural experiment yet observes a SUSTAINED minimum wage far above ~60% of the LOCAL median; the disemployment "turning point" is theoretically motivated and shows up for very large steps, but its precise location is extrapolated, not directly measured.
  - Threshold/heterogeneity estimates are statistically fragile and vulnerable to false discovery (Dube-Lindner caveat); the -1.01 high-bite OWE hinges on cutoff and covariate choices.
  - Longer-run dynamics (firm entry/exit, capital-labor substitution over 5-10+ years) are under-identified; most credible designs are medium-run.
  - No unifying model reconciles flat employment + full price pass-through + non-falling profits + productivity gains; monopsony explains one fact, competition another.
  - Within "flat aggregate headcount," losses to specific subgroups (older/low-skill/automatable/tradable) are real but under-powered — the distribution of harm is less well measured than the average.
  - The strongest US negative case (Seattle) remains contested on method (data coverage of multi-site employers; controls) and its interpretation (hours/hiring vs. headcount).
