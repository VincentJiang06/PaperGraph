# S05 — Caravello, McKay & Wolf (2025), MIT/FRB Minneapolis/NBER, "Evaluating Monetary Policy Counterfactuals: (When) Do We Need Structural Models?"
URL: https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/mp_modelcnfctls.pdf
(PDF read directly, pages 1-2. Load-bearing for the causal-identification line.)

Verbatim quotes:
- Abstract: "To evaluate the evolution of the macro-economy under alternative assumptions on monetary policy, it suffices, under weak structural assumptions, to know the causal effects of monetary shocks on macroeconomic outcomes. The existing empirical literature estimates the effects of monetary shocks to the short end of the yield curve, thus allowing the evaluation of counterfactuals that change assumptions on near-term policy. If the contemplated policy changes are instead more persistent, then model structure becomes necessary, for one sole purpose: to extrapolate from the estimated short-end effects to those of policy shocks further out on the yield curve."
- Abstract: "Among popular models of monetary policy transmission, market incompleteness (i.e., "HANK") does not change this extrapolation much, while behavioral frictions do."
- Intro: "How would the macro-economy have evolved if monetary policy had been conducted differently? Following Lucas (1976), the standard way to evaluate such counterfactuals has been to go fully structural—i.e., build a model of macroeconomic fluctuations, change monetary policy in that model, and finally re-solve it."
- Intro: "The key challenge limiting the applicability of this approach is that, in principle, it requires evidence on the effects of policy shocks along the entire yield curve—something the empirical literature clearly fails to deliver."

Relevance: QE acts on the LONG end / via persistent shocks — exactly the region where the directly-estimable ("sufficient statistics") empirical evidence runs out and answers become model-dependent extrapolations.
