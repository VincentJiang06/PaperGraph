# S03 — Hutchins Center on Fiscal & Monetary Policy at Brookings (June 2015), media summary of three papers (Bivens; Doepke–Selezneva–Schneider; Beraja–Fuster–Hurst–Vavra)
URL: https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/QE-and-Inequality-Media-Summary.pdf
Title: "Little Evidence that Fed's Unconventional Monetary Policies Increased Income Inequality"
(PDF read directly, pages 1-2.)

Verbatim quotes:
- Header subtitle: "QE reduced inequality by lowering unemployment and increasing house prices, but effects uneven with metro areas that were hardest hit by recession benefitting least"
- "The monetary policies deployed by the Federal Reserve during the recession reduced inequality by pushing the economy closer to full employment, but it may have had uneven impacts, with middle class homeowners benefitting the most but areas with large drops in house prices the least"
- "Bivens finds that the direct impact on inequality of monetary policy seems quite small—finding it unclear if it has been slightly progressive or slightly regressive."
- Doepke, Selezneva & Schneider "challenge the conventional wisdom that the wealthy gained the most from expansionary monetary policy, finding that when the Federal Reserve aims for higher inflation, it is the middle-aged, middle-class households who benefit at the expense of wealthy retirees, and that the poor and young households are the least affected."
- Doepke et al.: "borrowers (those with mortgages) benefit from higher inflation, while others lose."
- Beraja, Hurst & Vavra: "expansionary monetary policy helped stimulate the U.S. economy overall during the Great Recession, but widened the disparities among regions, with the Fed's first round of quantitative easing (QE1) providing the least amount of monetary stimulus to the metro areas hit hardest by the recession."
- Beraja et al.: "mortgage refinancing increased the most in places that needed it the least—places with the fewest underwater homeowners."
