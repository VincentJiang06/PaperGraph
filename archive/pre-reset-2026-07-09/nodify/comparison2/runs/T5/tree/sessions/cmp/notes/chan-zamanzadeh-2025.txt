Method: continuous threshold regression ("regression kink with unknown thresholds") applied
to U.S. state-level panel data, 1993-2016, to estimate an employment "tipping point" as a
function of the minimum-to-median-wage ratio (effectively the Kaitz index).

Reported finding (per search-engine synthesis of the abstract): "The tipping occurs at
50-55% of the state's median wage among women, and 40-45% among men." The marginal
employment effect is reported to be near-zero or mildly negative below this ratio, and
"considerably more negative" above it -- i.e., a nonlinear, threshold-shaped (not smoothly
increasing) relationship between the minimum-to-median wage ratio and the disemployment
effect, with the threshold differing by gender. Simulations of minimum wage reforms in the
paper reportedly show "nonlinear and asymmetric employment effects."

Cross-check: this threshold-shaped pattern is directionally consistent with, though from an
independent methodology than, the Clemens & Strain (2021) finding (see DOC-0001 /
dube-lindner-2024.txt) that six states raising minimums by $2.50/hour or more (implying
larger jumps in the minimum-to-median ratio) had OWE=-1.01, versus OWE=+0.46 for states
raising by less -- both point to a convex, threshold-like rather than linear dose-response
relationship between the size of the increase (relative to the wage distribution) and the
disemployment effect, even though Cengiz et al. (2019) and Godoey & Reich (2021) did not
detect such heterogeneity in their (different, pre-2016 or sub-state) samples.
