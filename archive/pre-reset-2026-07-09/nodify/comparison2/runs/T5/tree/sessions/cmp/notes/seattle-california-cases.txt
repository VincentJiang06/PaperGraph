SOURCE A: Ekaterina Jardim, Mark C. Long, Robert D. Plotnick, Emma van Inwegen, Jacob
Vigdor, Hilary Wething (2017/2022), "Minimum Wage Increases, Wages, and Low-Wage
Employment: Evidence from Seattle", NBER Working Paper No. 23532 (published American
Economic Journal: Applied Economics / AEA 2022).
URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532

Seattle raised its minimum wage from $9.47 to as much as $11 in 2015, and to as much as $13
in 2016 (large employers). Key findings extracted via secondary summary of the paper's
results (WebFetch synthesis of NBER page, cross-checked against multiple secondary sources):
- For the second increase to $13/hour: "Hours worked in low-wage jobs declined by 6-7
  percent" while "hourly wages rose by 3 percent."
- Total payroll for such low-wage jobs decreased, "resulting in workers earning an average
  of $74 per month per job" less in 2016 than they otherwise would have.
- The first increase to $11 "showed more modest effects."
- Using a narrower, industry-based definition (the restaurant sector specifically, closer to
  the classic Card-Krueger design), the study found "an effect of zero," consistent with
  many earlier studies -- i.e., the negative result only appears when using the broader
  "jobs paying below a specified real hourly wage rate" definition (which is sensitive to
  wage growth pushing jobs out of the "low-wage" bin), not the narrower single-industry
  definition.
- Longitudinal analysis of individual Seattle workers matched to counterparts in outlying
  regions found "no change in the probability of continued employment but significant
  reductions in hours, particularly for less experienced workers." Job turnover and hiring
  of new workers into low-wage jobs declined.
This finding (hours effect concentrated in a broadly-defined, high-turnover low-wage
population, not in continued employment or in the narrowly defined restaurant sector) has
been contested on methodological grounds by Berkeley IRLE researchers (Allegretto, Godoey,
Reich) using a synthetic-control comparison group restricted to the restaurant sector, who
report no significant employment effect; this is a well-known, unresolved methodological
dispute over the correct low-wage sample/definition and control group for city-level policy
evaluation.

SOURCE B: The Shift Project (Daniel Schneider, Kristen Harknett et al.), "Early Effects of
California's $20 Fast Food Minimum Wage: Large Wage Increases with No Effects on Hours,
Scheduling, or Benefits", Harvard Kennedy School / UCSF, October 2024.
URL: https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/ca_fastfood_MW_Final.pdf

[KEY POINTS, verbatim from brief]
"California fast food workers experienced substantial wage increases. Immediately after the
new minimum wage went into effect, hourly wages for California fast food workers increased
by at least $2.50, and the share of California fast food workers earning less than $20/hour
declined by about 60 percentage points."
"We find no evidence that wage increases had unintended consequences on staffing,
scheduling, or wage theft. In response to the sizeable wage increase for California fast
food workers, we do not find evidence that employers turned to understaffing or reduced
scheduled work hours to offset the increased labor costs. Rather, weekly work hours stayed
about the same for California fast food workers, and levels of understaffing appeared to
ease. We also find that unstable scheduling practices and wage theft were unaffected by the
wage increase."
"We find no evidence that wage increases were accompanied by a reduction in fringe benefits.
... We find no evidence of reductions in any of 7 types of fringe benefits in response to
the California fast food wage increase."
Methodology: event-study models using Shift Project survey data from 3,420 California fast
food workers plus comparison samples of retail workers in California and fast-food/retail
workers in other Western states at the same 136 large firms, comparing hourly fast food
workers at covered California firms before/after April 1, 2024 to workers at the exact same
firms in neighboring western states.
The brief notes this is "the largest instantaneous increase ever implemented by a state
government" ($4/hour, from $16 to $20), and situates it against the unresolved Seattle
dispute: "The Seattle evidence then is unsettled and, further, the minimum wage increase in
California is distinct in that it is industry-specific, statewide, and even larger."

SOURCE C: Jeffrey Clemens, Olivia Edwards, Jonathan Meer (2025), "Did California's Fast Food
Minimum Wage Reduce Employment?", NBER Working Paper No. 34033, July 2025.
URL: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34033/w34033.pdf

[ABSTRACT, verbatim]
"We analyze the effect of California's $20 fast food minimum wage, which was enacted in
September 2023 and went into effect in April 2024, on employment in the fast food sector. In
unadjusted data from the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, we find that employment
in California's fast food sector declined by 2.7 percent relative to employment in the fast
food sector elsewhere in the United States from September 2023 through September 2024.
Adjusting for pre-AB 1228 trends increases this differential decline to 3.2 percent, while
netting out the equivalent employment changes in non-minimum-wage-intensive industries
further increases the decline. Our median estimate translates into a loss of 18,000 jobs in
California's fast food sector relative to the counterfactual."

NOTE ON DIVERGENCE: Sources B and C study the *same* policy episode (California AB 1228,
$20/hour fast food minimum effective April 2024) using different data (worker-survey panel
at 136 named firms vs. QCEW administrative payroll counts) and different outcome definitions
(hours/scheduling/benefits vs. headcount employment), and reach opposite headline
conclusions on the employment margin specifically -- directly reproducing, in a single 2024
episode, the same Card-Krueger-vs-competitive-theory methodological fault line documented
for Seattle in Source A.
