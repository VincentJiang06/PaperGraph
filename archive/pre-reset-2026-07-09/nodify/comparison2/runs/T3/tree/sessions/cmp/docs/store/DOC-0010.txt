European Central Bank. "Monetary policy and inequality." ECB Economic Bulletin, Issue 2/2021.
URL: https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/articles/2021/html/ecb.ebart202102_01~1773181511.en.html

KEY EXTRACTED STATEMENTS (via WebFetch summary of the article; euro-area focus):
- "The easing of monetary policy would seem, overall, to have dampened economic inequality in recent years."
- "The easing of monetary policy clearly has an inequality-reducing impact via its indirect effects, resulting in increased employment for lower-income households in particular."
- On income: lowest-income households' mean income rose "more than 3%, while those of other quintiles increase by around 0.5%" in response to the policy easing episodes studied.
- On wealth: "When the effects on stock prices and house prices are combined, the Gini coefficient of net wealth -- a broad measure of inequality -- remains broadly unchanged." Higher stock prices "tends, by itself, to lead to an increase in wealth inequality," while rising house prices have offsetting effects since housing is more broadly distributed in the euro area than equities.

Relevance / caveat: This is a Euro-area analysis, not the US. It shows that when housing is broadly enough held (as in much of continental Europe, with higher homeownership and lower equity-market participation than the US), the house-price offset to the equity-price effect can leave aggregate wealth Gini roughly flat, while the employment channel actively reduces INCOME inequality. This is an important cross-country data point for the identification/generalizability question (N-0012): the same QE "technology" can have a more equalizing net effect in economies with different asset-ownership structures (more broad-based housing wealth, less concentrated equity ownership) than in the US, where equity ownership is more concentrated at the top and housing plays a comparatively smaller offsetting role for the wealthiest. It also shows the employment/income channel and the wealth-concentration channel move somewhat independently: income inequality can fall while wealth inequality stays flat or rises, meaning the employment channel does not straightforwardly "cancel out" the asset-price channel in inequality-metric terms -- they affect different distributions (income vs wealth) that are related but not mechanically identical.
