# Research Log — QE (2008–2021) and Wealth Inequality in Developed Economies

## Question
QE 在多大程度上是发达经济体(以美国为主)财富不平等扩大的"原因",而非人口老龄化、技术偏向性进步、全球化等结构性力量在反事实情形下本来也会造成的结果?
区分:(A) 资产价格渠道 (B) 储蓄者/借款者再分配渠道 (C) 宏观(就业/工资)渠道。
直面因果识别难题。给出有校准的判断。

## Viewpoints & Claims (plan)

### V1 资产价格渠道 (asset-price channel widens wealth gaps)
- C1.1 QE 是否显著推高资产价格(股票/债券/房产)?(event studies)
- C1.2 资产持有是否高度集中于富裕阶层,使资产价格上涨机械地扩大财富差距?
- ADV1 反事实下(无QE)资产价格是否也会因"储蓄过剩/人口结构/全球资本过剩"而上涨?(Mian-Straub-Sufi savings glut of the rich)

### V2 储蓄者/借款者再分配渠道 (saver/borrower redistribution)
- C2.1 低利率如何在净储蓄者和净债务人之间再分配?净储蓄者/净债务人在财富/收入分布中的位置?
- C2.2 微观异质性模型(HANK, Auclert 2019)对该渠道方向和大小的估计
- ADV2 该渠道是否实际上对下层/中层(净债务人,如房贷持有者)有利,从而部分抵消资产价格渠道?

### V3 宏观(就业/工资)渠道
- C3.1 QE 是否显著改善了就业/降低失业率,尤其是对低收入劳动者?
- C3.2 若无QE,失业率路径的反事实估计如何?(Fed/BIS DSGE反事实模拟)
- ADV3 该渠道对底层收入(flow)有利,是否足以抵消资产价格渠道对财富(stock)不平等的影响?——区分"收入不平等" vs "财富不平等"

### V4 结构性力量的反事实基准
- C4.1 财富集中度(top 1% wealth share)在2008年前的长期趋势如何?QE是否只是延续了既有趋势?
- C4.2 有无研究将财富不平等上升分解为:资本利得 vs 储蓄率差异 vs 劳动收入不平等(SBTC/全球化)贡献?
- ADV4 若结构性趋势能解释危机前的相似斜率,则QE的边际贡献存疑

### V5 因果识别难题
- C5.1 央行/学术界用什么方法估计QE对不平等的因果效应?(反事实DSGE模拟、跨国比较、微观异质性模型)
- C5.2 这些方法的内在局限/批评是什么?(内生性、反事实假设本身依赖模型、无QE=更深衰退的对照难以观测)
- C5.3 是否有综述文献试图给出net/calibrated判断?

## Search log / source notes

- S1 Bank of England (Bell, Joyce, Liu, Young 2012) "The Distributional Effects of Asset Purchases": primary-source quantification for UK QE1+QE2. Key verbatim: "holdings are heavily skewed with the top 5% of households holding 40% of these assets" (financial wealth ex-pension). Also: "Bank's assessment is that asset purchases have pushed up the price of equities by at least as much as they have pushed up the price of gilts." Crucially frames explicit counterfactual: "Without the Bank's asset purchases, most people... would have been worse off. Economic growth would have been lower. Unemployment would have been higher." Also shows QE's effect on savers is dominated by Bank Rate, not QE itself: "Changes in Bank Rate – not asset purchases – have been the dominant influence on the interest households receive." Pension funds: QE roughly neutral for fully-funded schemes (assets and liabilities both rise); only deficit schemes hurt, and the deficit-driver "was not caused by QE" but by pre-existing equity/gilt price divergence.

- S2 Auclert (2019 AER) "Monetary Policy and the Redistribution Channel": theoretical microfoundation for saver/borrower channel. Distinguishes earnings-heterogeneity, Fisher (inflation), and interest-rate-exposure (URE) channels. Key: "agents whose financial wealth is primarily invested in short-term certificates of deposit tend to have positive UREs... those with large long-term bond investments or adjustable-rate mortgage liabilities tend to have negative UREs. Real interest rate falls redistribute away from the first group towards the second group." But also: "With long asset maturities, lower real interest rates can benefit asset holders with lower MPCs and make interest rate cuts less effective..." → shows the redistribution channel is ambiguous in sign/magnitude and depends on balance-sheet duration structure, not simply "savers lose, debtors gain."

- S3 Mian, Straub, Sufi (NBER WP26941, "Saving Glut of the Rich"): structural/endogeneity evidence. "Since the 1980s, saving by the top 1% has surged, creating a saving glut of the rich... as important in understanding key trends such as the persistent decline in the natural rate of interest." Implication: low r* (which QE operationalizes/reinforces) is partly *caused by* rising income inequality (itself driven by SBTC/globalization/tax changes), not purely exogenous Fed action → reverse-causality complicates "QE → inequality" causal story.

- S4 Domanski/Scatigna/Zabai (BIS QR 2016) "Wealth inequality and monetary policy": simulation decomposing asset-price channel into bonds/rates (negligible), equities (dis-equalizing, richer households own more), housing (equalizing, poorer households own more, "partly offsets"). Explicit long-run baseline: top-1% wealth share data 1810-2010 shows "inequality has been increasing since the 1980s" — i.e., decades before QE, reversing a 20th-century declining trend. Explicit methodology caveats: "simulation is only a partial equilibrium exercise"; excludes human capital/unemployment channel, i.e. likely OVERSTATES QE's dis-equalizing effect by omitting the macro channel.

- S5 Bivens (2015, Hutchins/Brookings WP12) "Gauging the Impact of the Fed on Inequality...": the "compared to what" framing — vs. equivalent-sized fiscal stimulus, QE's distributional effect is small/ambiguous (housing gains ~equalizing offset equity gains ~dis-equalizing, "these effects are largely offsetting"); vs. no-stimulus-at-all counterfactual, QE is "strongly progressive" via employment, because low-wage workers' wages are far more sensitive to the unemployment rate (citing Katz-Krueger, Baker-Bernstein, Gregg-Machin decile wage-Phillips-curve literature) — own regression confirms 10th-percentile wage growth most cyclically sensitive.

- S6 Saez & Zucman (2020 JEP): "By any metric, the period from 1980 to 2020 has been an era of extraordinary wealth accumulation among the rich" — top 1% wealth-to-income ratio went from 60 to 200 years of average income, and this is a 40-year trend, not a post-2008 phenomenon → strong baseline for the "would have happened anyway" counterfactual argument.

- S7 Engen/Laubach/Reifschneider (Fed FEDS 2015): DSGE/FRB-US model-based counterfactual simulation of combined QE+forward guidance for the US: "peak unemployment effect—subtracting 1¼ percentage points from the unemployment rate relative to what would have occurred in the absence of the unconventional policy actions." Gives concrete magnitude for macro channel, but explicitly model-dependent (methodology = internal counterfactual simulation, not a natural experiment) — illustrates the identification problem directly: the counterfactual is a model output, not observed.

- S8 Montecino & Epstein (2017, INET/PERI) "Did Quantitative Easing Increase Income Inequality?": the most directly on-point empirical study — decomposes US SCF income changes pre-QE vs QE period into employment / asset-return / mortgage-refinancing channels via RIF-Oaxaca-Blinder, THEN builds an explicit counterfactual causal exercise. Findings: "employment changes and mortgage refinancing were equalizing, [but] these impacts were nonetheless swamped by the large dis-equalizing effects of asset appreciations." Net calibrated verdict: "QE led to modest increases in inequality despite having some positive impacts on employment and mortgage refinancing" / "the impact of quantitative easing on the distribution of income was at least modestly regressive." Explicitly flags the identification problem: "these decomposition results cannot be interpreted causally" without assumptions on counterfactual employment/stock-price paths; conclusion is sensitive to those assumptions ("for QE to have actually decreased inequality... necessary to either strongly downplay the potential impact of QE on stock returns or assume very large employment effects").

- S9 Federal Reserve FEDS Notes (Aug 2021) "Wealth Inequality and COVID-19": official Distributional Financial Accounts data for the 2020-21 QE episode. "asset-price increases ('revaluations') were the dominant source of wealth accumulation, accounting for nearly 80%" of the $18tn wealth gain. Yet: "In total, changes in the concentration of wealth were not large in magnitude. The share of wealth going to the Top 1%... increased 2.2 percentage points in the Rebound... Bottom 50... share only increasing by 0.2 percentage points." → complicates simplistic "QE mechanically = more inequality" story: even a huge equity-driven wealth rebound moved top-1%/bottom-50% *shares* only modestly, partly because bottom-50 gained proportionally fastest off a small base (real estate + deposits), even though absolute/dollar gains were extremely skewed.

## Claim conclusions

- C1.1/C1.2 (asset-price channel exists & concentrated): SUPPORTED. S1, S4, S9 all confirm QE materially raises equity/bond/housing prices and financial-asset ownership is skewed to top of distribution (top 5% = 40% of financial assets, S1; top 10% own most equities, S9). Lean: this channel is real and dis-equalizing in direction, robustly across UK and US evidence.
- ADV1 (counterfactual asset-price rise without QE): MIXED/CONTEXT. S3 (MSS) shows structural saving-glut-of-the-rich was independently pushing down r* and up asset valuations since the 1980s — some of the "low-rate, high-valuation" world QE operated in was already emerging for reasons unrelated to QE. Weakens a maximalist "QE caused the valuation gains" reading, but does not refute that QE added a further boost on top (S1's ~16% household net-wealth boost estimate; S9's ~80% revaluation share).
- C2.1/C2.2 (saver/borrower channel): MIXED, small magnitude. S2 (Auclert) shows the sign is not simply "poor debtor gains, rich saver loses" — it depends on balance-sheet duration (URE), and long-duration asset holders (typically wealthier) can also benefit from rate cuts via valuation, partially offsetting the equalizing flow-redistribution. S1's UK numbers show the direct income-flow effect (~£70bn less deposit interest vs ~£100bn less debt-service cost) is an order of magnitude smaller than the ~£600bn wealth/valuation effect — i.e. this channel is second-order next to the asset-price channel.
- ADV2: not strongly refuting — the channel is at most mildly equalizing (S1, S8's "mortgage refinancing" component was equalizing but small: contributed +2.8pp to median income growth in S8, far smaller than the -6pp(ish) dis-equalizing pull from equity returns on the 95/10 ratio).
- C3.1/C3.2 (macro/employment channel): SUPPORTED as equalizing, moderate magnitude. S5 and S8 both show low-wage workers' pay is disproportionately sensitive to slack; S7 gives a concrete US counterfactual magnitude (-1.25pp unemployment peak from unconventional policy). S8's decomposition: employment channel was equalizing but "swamped" by the equity channel.
- ADV3 (does employment channel dominate?): REFUTED on net for the wealth measure specifically (S8, S9), though S5 argues it dominates for *income*-based measures / vs a no-stimulus counterfactual. Key distinction preserved in article: wealth-stock inequality (equity channel wins) vs income/wage-flow inequality (employment channel wins or is closer to neutral).
- C4.1/C4.2 (structural, pre-existing trend): SUPPORTED strongly. S6, S4 both show top wealth-share concentration is a 40+ year (1980-2020) trend that predates QE by decades and continued through non-QE periods too; S3 links this to SBTC/globalization-driven income inequality (via higher saving rates of top earners), independent of monetary policy.
- ADV4: no strong evidence found that the pre-2008 slope and 2008-2021 slope are statistically indistinguishable (data limitation - did not find a formal break-test study); treat as an open question / uncertainty in the calibrated verdict rather than a settled refutation.
- C5.1/C5.2/C5.3 (identification): SUPPORTED — genuine hard problem, multiple documented strategies (model-based DSGE/FRB-US counterfactual simulation S7; micro decomposition + counterfactual bounding S8; partial-equilibrium valuation simulation with explicit caveats S4) but none is a clean natural experiment; results are sensitive to counterfactual assumptions (S8 explicit sensitivity discussion); "compared to what" baseline flips the sign of the normative conclusion (S5).

## Root conclusion (thesis)
QE's causal contribution to 2008-2021 wealth-inequality widening is REAL, CHANNEL-SPECIFIC, and MODEST relative to structural forces: dis-equalizing via the equity-price channel (largest, most robust effect), roughly second-order/ambiguous via the saver-borrower channel, and equalizing via the employment channel — netting to "modestly dis-equalizing" for wealth-stock measures per the best available decomposition (S8), while the identification problem (no observed counterfactual; model/assumption-dependent estimates; QE partly endogenous to the same structural forces that raised inequality via r*) means this net estimate has a wide and appropriately humble confidence band, and should not be read as QE explaining more than a fraction of the four-decade structural rise in top wealth shares (S6).
