Title: Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor
Author(s): Daron Acemoglu, Pascual Restrepo
Source: NBER Working Paper No. 25684
URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w25684
Accessed via: WebFetch, 2026-07-09

Verbatim key quotes:

"Automation, which enables capital to replace labor in tasks it was previously engaged in, shifts the task content of production against labor because of a displacement effect."

This displacement effect "always reduces the labor share in value added and may reduce labor demand even as it raises productivity."

"The introduction of new tasks changes the task content of production in favor of labor because of a reinstatement effect, and always raises the labor share and labor demand."

On recent decades: slower employment growth is explained by "an acceleration in the displacement effect, especially in manufacturing, a weaker reinstatement effect, and slower growth of productivity than in previous decades."

Notes: This is the canonical task-based framework distinguishing task-level substitution (displacement) from job/labor-share-level net outcomes (which also depend on the reinstatement effect — new tasks/jobs created alongside automation). It implies net employment effects are NOT a mechanical readout of task-exposure estimates (like S3/S4): the same technical displacement potential can coexist with stable, growing, or shrinking net employment depending on the strength of new-task creation. This is theoretically important for resisting the inference "80% of tasks are exposed" implies "net job loss."
