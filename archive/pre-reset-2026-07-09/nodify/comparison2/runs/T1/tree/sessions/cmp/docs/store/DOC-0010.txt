Source A: David Autor (2015), "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation," Journal of Economic Perspectives 29(3).
URL: https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/why%20are%20there%20still%20jobs%202014.pdf

Key argument (paraphrased/extracted, consistent with the well-established literature): automation both substitutes for and complements labor. Focusing only on substitution misses the central mechanism: automating a subset of a job's tasks raises the economic VALUE of the tasks workers uniquely retain (comparative advantage), and — if product demand is elastic — lowers unit costs enough to expand output and thus expand demand for the remaining human tasks (the ATM/bank-teller mechanism above is a direct illustration). Computers substitute for workers in routine, codifiable tasks while amplifying comparative advantage in problem-solving, adaptability, judgment, and interpersonal tasks. The net employment effect of automating a task bundle depends jointly on: (a) the elasticity of demand for the task's output, (b) the elasticity of labor supply for the complementary tasks, and (c) the share of the total job that is actually automated versus left to humans.

Source B: Daron Acemoglu (2024), "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI," NBER Working Paper 32487.
URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487

Key finding (extracted from search aggregation of the paper and its coverage): using a task-based model and existing estimates of AI task-exposure and task-level cost savings, Acemoglu estimates AI will produce only a "modest increase" in US GDP — approximately 1.1% to 1.6% over the next decade (roughly 0.05 percentage points of additional annual TFP growth), because he estimates only about 5% of tasks in the economy will be profitably performed by AI within that timeframe. He explicitly contrasts this with much larger forecasts from Korinek & Suh (2024, ~100% GDP growth over 10 years in a baseline AGI scenario) and from Goldman Sachs / McKinsey Global Institute (still-large but smaller than AGI-optimist forecasts).

Relevance: Together these two sources provide the THEORETICAL reason to expect the currently-observed "mild" employment data to be the correct long-run reading rather than an artifact of measurement lag — IF one accepts Acemoglu's low estimate of the profitably-automatable task share. This is a genuine rival explanation to the "lag" hypothesis explored in N-0014/N-0015: the data could look mild simply because the true structural effect, given task-level economics (elasticity + automatable-task-share), IS modest — not because a larger effect is coming but hasn't shown up yet. Note Acemoglu's estimate is itself contested (Korinek/Suh, Goldman Sachs argue for substantially larger effects), so this is a genuine, unresolved disagreement among serious economists, not a settled fact.
