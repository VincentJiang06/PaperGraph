Source: Congressional Research Service (CRS), "The Role of Employer Reports in Understanding Labor Markets" (Congress.gov, IF13238); BLS Contingent Work Supplement (CWS) revision proposal coverage.
URL: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF13238

Key points (extracted via search aggregation):
- Gig/platform work undercount: "A 2023 Pew Research Center study suggested that 16 percent of Americans have earned money through online gig platforms, but official BLS statistics have been lower due to methodological limits." BLS has proposed revisions to its Contingent Work Supplement (CWS) — including non-employed respondents and a longer reference period — specifically because the existing survey design is known to undercount platform/gig participation.
- Classification and revision lag: "Limitations of employer-provided data include the timeliness of published data and the contemporaneousness of classification systems. Annual benchmark revisions can revise total employment by hundreds of thousands of jobs after a year of processing," and in fast-moving sectors (explicitly including technology), "shifts can render the numbers a year or two out of shape" before official data catches up.
- AI-specific measurement gap: commentators have suggested BLS's Occupational Requirements Survey (ORS) could be expanded to more directly capture how job task content is changing as generative AI is deployed in workplaces — implying the current official toolkit was not designed with AI-driven task reallocation in mind and does not directly measure it.

Relevance: This substantiates N-0016's core question — official US labor statistics (CPS/BLS survey infrastructure) have well-documented, methodologically-acknowledged blind spots specifically in the areas most relevant to how AI-driven labor adjustment would first show up: gig/platform work, fast-moving tech-sector reclassification, and within-job task-content change. Combined with the Yale Budget Lab's own self-described "small fire vs. burning house" limitation (DOC-0007), there is a real, non-speculative case that a portion of any early AI-driven adjustment would NOT be visible in the topline series used to characterize the labor market as "mild."
