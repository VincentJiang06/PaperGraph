Source A: Robert Solow (1987 book review, NYT) — origin of the "productivity paradox"; Brynjolfsson, Rock, Syverson, "The Productivity J-Curve: How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies," NBER Working Paper 25148.
URL: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25148/w25148.pdf

Verbatim: Solow's famous line — one "can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." Solow separately noted that "a technological revolution, a drastic change in our productive lives" had curiously been accompanied by "a slowing-down of productivity growth, not by a step up."

Brynjolfsson/Rock/Syverson's explanation ("J-Curve"): "General purpose technologies (GPTs) are engines for growth" but their productivity payoff is systematically MISMEASURED in the early period, because firms must first make large IINTANGIBLE investments (new business processes, organizational redesign, worker retraining, complementary software/data) that standard national accounts do not capture as investment. Measured total factor productivity therefore first DIPS (intangible investment costs are expensed, not capitalized, in official statistics) and only later RISES once the intangible capital stock starts showing up in measured output — hence a "J" shape: apparent stagnation followed by a later acceleration. This is a structural, well-documented reason why GPT-driven productivity/employment effects are systematically under-measured in early years, independent of whether the "true" effect is large or small.

Historical precedent cited: the technologies of the British Industrial Revolution produced "Engels' Pause" — roughly half a century of capital accumulation and industrial innovation accompanied by stagnant wages before broad-based gains appeared.

Source B: Paul David (1990), "The Dynamo and the Computer: An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox," American Economic Review P&P; extended to ChatGPT by James Pethokoukis, AEI, "The Dynamo, the Computer, and ChatGPT: Explaining Today's Productivity Paradox."
URL: https://www.aei.org/articles/the-dynamo-the-computer-and-chatgpt-explaining-todays-productivity-paradox/

Verbatim: "It would take until the 1920s for those measures of diffusion for lighting and motors to hit 50 percent and have an impact on productivity growth" — i.e., roughly a 30-40 year lag from the initial dynamo/electric-motor breakthroughs (c.1880s) to measurable factory-level productivity gains (electrification accounted for about half of all US manufacturing productivity growth in the 1920s, per earlier search-aggregated findings). "Productivity growth will be undermeasured when businesses adopt an innovation because to extract value, companies need to devote capital" to the "co-invention of new processes, products, business models and human capital" — i.e. the gain came not from swapping the power source (steam engine for dynamo) but from later REDESIGNING the entire factory floor layout around distributed electric motors, which took roughly a generation of organizational learning.

On today's AI specifically, the AEI piece is explicitly cautious and does NOT commit to a specific number of years, quoting Brynjolfsson's own view that the economy may be "approaching the rising part of the productivity J-curve for AI" while stressing "patience is still required" — i.e. even the researcher most associated with the "it's coming" camp declines to give a precise ETA.

Relevance: This is the strongest available historical evidence FOR the "effects are lagged, not absent" hypothesis (N-0005/N-0014) — with a specific, well-documented historical lag length (roughly 30-50 years for electrification; the IT productivity paradox of the 1970s-1990s took about 15-20 years to resolve). It also provides a STRUCTURAL, non-hand-wavy mechanism (mismeasurement of intangible investment) for why the lag would show up as literally invisible in current statistics rather than merely "small." However, it is symmetric evidence: it establishes that lags of this kind are real and historically well-precedented, but does NOT by itself distinguish whether AI's specific lag will be short (a few years, given AI's software-only, low-fixed-capital nature) or long (decades, given the electrification precedent) — that requires N-0015's leading-indicator approach.
