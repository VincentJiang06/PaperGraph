Source: Revelio Labs x Ramp, "Does AI Cause Job Losses? New Data Says Heavy Adopters Hire More" (2025/2026).
URL: https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/ai-and-work/greater-ai-investment-more-hiring/

Verbatim key findings:
"Among high-intensity adopters, the share of entry-level workers increased by 1.15 percentage points relative to companies that had not yet adopted AI." (low-intensity adopters saw modest declines in junior staff share.)
"High-intensity adopters maintain employment levels roughly 10.2% higher than companies that had not yet adopted AI, while low-intensity adopters show no statistically significant employment gains."

Methodology: Ramp corporate-card/bill-pay transaction data (identifying AI vendor purchases) linked to Revelio Labs workforce data (built from 100M+ public employment profiles, covering roughly two-thirds of the US employed population) across 21,000+ US companies. AI "adoption" defined as ≥3 consecutive months of ≥$100/month AI vendor spend (to filter out mere experimentation). Companies split into high-intensity (top third of AI spend per employee) vs low-intensity adopters; event-study design comparing adopters to not-yet-adopted, otherwise-similar future adopters (to reduce confounding from pre-existing firm differences).

Explicit caveat from the authors: "It remains too early to draw conclusions about the long-run effects of AI on the labor market" — this captures only early adoption-period patterns.

Relevance / critical tension with N-0009/N-0011: this is exactly the kind of independent, high-frequency, non-official alternative data source N-0017 asks for — and its headline finding runs in the OPPOSITE direction from the Stanford "Canaries" ADP-based finding (16% relative decline in youth employment in AI-exposed OCCUPATIONS). The two are not strictly contradictory (Revelio/Ramp measures FIRM-level AI adoption intensity and finds AI-adopting firms overall hire/grow more — plausibly reflecting reverse causality/selection: healthier, growing firms are the ones with budget and appetite to adopt AI tools — whereas Stanford/ADP measures OCCUPATION-level AI exposure regardless of which specific firm employs the worker, and finds the youngest workers in automation-leaning occupations losing ground even as overall firm employment may be flat or growing). This divergence is itself an important, honest finding for calibration: high-frequency alternative data sources do NOT uniformly point toward "AI is quietly destroying jobs beneath the official radar" — some point the opposite way, and the discrepancy is best explained by firm-level selection/reverse-causality effects rather than a clean confirmation of either the "masking" hypothesis or its negation.
