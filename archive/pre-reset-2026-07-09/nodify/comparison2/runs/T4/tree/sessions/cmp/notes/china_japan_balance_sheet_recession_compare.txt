Source: Forbes (George Calhoun), "China's Economic Crisis Is Tracking Japan's Downturn In The 1990s" (Feb 2025); Nomura/Richard Koo commentary on China deflation and balance sheet recession
URLs: https://www.forbes.com/sites/georgecalhoun/2025/02/15/chinas-economic-crisis-is-tracking-japans-downturn-in-the-1990s/ ; https://www.nomuraconnects.com/focused-thinking-posts/as-advanced-nations-fight-inflation-china-grapples-deflation-says-richard-koo/

Key extracted points:
- "The CSI 300 — considered the Chinese equivalent of the S&P 500 — is down 36% from its all-time peak four years ago" (as of the article's writing); the author finds this decline pattern correlates strongly with Japan's Nikkei 225 plunge from its December 1989 peak.
- Housing inventory crisis: "China's unsold inventory of housing would amount to RMB 93 trillion ($13 trillion)" against annual sales of roughly RMB 9 trillion -- a backlog representing about 70% of China's current GDP, versus roughly 7% of GDP for comparable US unsold housing inventory during the 2007-08 subprime crisis. (I.e., by this specific metric, China's real-estate overhang is proportionally an order of magnitude larger relative to GDP than the US subprime-crisis benchmark; the article uses this to argue China's imbalance could be more severe than Japan's, though it does not give Japan's own equivalent ratio directly.)
- Youth unemployment: China's rate "had risen continuously to a record high of more than 21 percent, as high as 40 percent in rural regions"; the article notes Japan's youth unemployment took 14 years to begin declining after its own crisis began, implying a potentially long adjustment period if China follows a similar path.
- Key currency divergence flagged as a major difference: China's currency (RMB) has weakened to multi-decade lows against the dollar, whereas the yen NEARLY DOUBLED in value during Japan's 1990s crisis (due to Plaza-Accord-era structural appreciation pressure). The article argues this is a meaningful divergence: a strengthening currency during crisis gave Japan more room for monetary/import-purchasing-power buffering, whereas a weakening RMB removes that particular buffer for China and could, by this specific channel, make China's adjustment potentially more difficult than Japan's classical "Japanification" pattern.
- Richard Koo (Nomura Chief Economist, originator of "balance sheet recession" concept from his study of Japan's 1990s bust) has explicitly characterized China's current deflationary dynamic (falling prices, construction-sector slump -- construction ~25% of China's GDP) as comparable to a Japan-style balance-sheet recession, with households/firms prioritizing debt paydown over new borrowing even near-zero rates -- though other analysts (e.g., some officials cited in Chinese state media) dispute that China's situation is a true "balance sheet recession" in Koo's technical sense.
