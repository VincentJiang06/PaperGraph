Source: Adam Tooze, "Chartbook 233: Whither China? Part 2: Posen v. Pettis or 'authoritarian impasse' v. 'structural dead-end'" (Adam Tooze's Chartbook substack, summarizing/quoting Adam Posen's Foreign Affairs essay "The End of China's Economic Miracle" and Michael Pettis's counter-argument)
URL: https://adamtooze.substack.com/p/chartbook-whither-china-part-ii-posen

Key points:
- Adam Posen (Peterson Institute president) argues China's slowdown reflects an "authoritarian impasse": authoritarian rule inherently undermines property rights, erodes business confidence, and weakens private investment incentives; per Tooze's summary of Posen, "only profound regime change offers China the chance of resuming sustained growth." This framing implies China's core problem is regime-type-specific and therefore CATEGORICALLY DIFFERENT from Japan's 1990s stagnation (a democratic, market-economy crisis) -- i.e., under Posen's view, directly transplanting the "Japanification" playbook/comparison to China could be misleading because the causal mechanism is different.
- Michael Pettis counters that the real driver is macroeconomic/structural: suppressed household income and excessive investment, i.e., a structural imbalance rooted in inequality rather than regime type. Crucially, per Tooze summarizing Pettis: "China's problems in many ways mirror those in export-dependent democracies like Germany or Japan in the 1980s and 1990s" -- explicitly drawing the Japan (and Germany) parallel as a structural-imbalance pattern (excess savings/investment, suppressed consumption, export dependence) that is NOT specific to authoritarian regimes, since it also occurred in a democratic Japan and Germany.
- Implication for N-0017 (does the "China vs Japan" binary framework itself hold up?): serious economists are unresolved and actively disagree on this exact question. Posen's view supports the adversarial claim that regime/scale differences make China's trajectory categorically different from Japan's (a "third path" driven by political-institutional causes, not just economic structure). Pettis's view supports the OPPOSITE: that the underlying structural mechanism (excess-savings/investment, suppressed household consumption, export dependence) is regime-agnostic and DOES make Japan (and Germany) a valid structural comparator regardless of China's different political system. There is no academic consensus resolving this; the binary "worse vs. better than Japan" framing is contested at the root, not just at the level of specific mechanisms.
